Beating the Myopic Oracle

A reinforcement learning agent chooses sequences of causal experiments. It is measured against a greedy expected-information-gain oracle that picks the single best next experiment every time. Across 23 seeds and three graph sizes, the agent wins on every one — on environments that were first proved to require experimentation at all.

What “gap closed” means

Performance is placed on a scale fixed by two reference policies measured on the same episodes: a random intervention policy at 0.0 and the greedy oracle at 1.0. A score above 1.0 means the agent identified the true causal graph in fewer experiments than the myopic optimum. The anchors are recomputed per run and checked by a canary, because a scale that drifts would make the headline number meaningless.

The margin narrows as the graph grows — +1.31 at d=4, +1.19 at d=5, +1.09 at d=6. That is the expected direction and is worth stating rather than hiding: as the hypothesis space grows the greedy oracle has more near-equivalent good moves available, so there is less room for planning to pay off. The trend is consistent enough across sizes that extrapolating it to much larger d would be unwise on this evidence alone.

Why the gates come first

An earlier version of this result was worthless

A previous round reported the agent beating greedy at d=6 by +1.098 to +1.145. Those numbers were retracted. The environment they were measured on could be solved without intervening at all in a large fraction of episodes, so “the agent chooses better experiments” was not what was being measured. Two gates now run before any result is allowed to count.

Gate 1 — the task must require experimenting

The rate at which the true graph is identified from observation alone must equal the fraction of graphs that are alone in their Markov equivalence class. That fraction is a predicted number, computed in closed form from the prior, not a threshold chosen after seeing data. Observed 0.085 / 0.060 / 0.060 against predicted 0.109 / 0.089 / 0.081.

Gate 2 — the choice of experiment must matter

A random policy must be clearly worse than the greedy oracle, with non-overlapping confidence intervals. If they tie, nothing rewards good experiment selection and there is nothing for an agent to learn.

Five further canaries run on every result: policy entropy must fall (a policy that never commits is not choosing), the two reference anchors must be exact and correctly ordered, the oracle must actually have a preference on a reasonable share of steps, seed spread must stay bounded, and Gate 1 must be recorded rather than assumed. A negative control configuration is run deliberately to make Gate 1 fail, confirming the gate can still detect the fault it was built for.

What matters, and what does not

The architecture is the whole story

A flat network that reads the belief state as one long vector cannot do this task: it scores −1.190, well below random. A per-node architecture, which shares parameters across nodes and treats the belief as structured rather than flat, scores +1.155 on identical configurations. That is a 2.3-point swing from architecture alone.

Nothing else moves the number at all

A 66-configuration sweep crossed 13 hyperparameters with both architectures. On the working architecture the largest effect of any lever is 0.288, and that one raises the difficulty of the success criterion, where a cost is expected. Everything else moves it by less than 0.2.

This is the most useful thing the sweep could have returned. The headline result is not a product of hyperparameter tuning: it survives learning rates spanning an order of magnitude, budgets from 10 to 40, training runs from 2,000 to 12,000 episodes, both graph priors, and every other lever tried. The comparison against greedy rests on the architecture and the environment, not on a search over settings.

Depth: a null worth keeping

A cheap offline probe predicted that a deeper message-passing network should help, and it did — by +0.06 on the probe. In the actual RL runs it did not: one round of message passing gave a worst-seed score of +1.144 with a spread of 0.096, while two rounds gave +0.989 with a spread of 0.310. Depth was kept at one.

The lesson recorded from this is about the probe, not the network: a static probe predicts differences in kind, not differences in degree. It correctly flags whether an architecture can represent the mapping, and says little about whether a policy gradient will find it.

Infrastructure — the enumeration wall

Everything above depends on an exact Bayesian posterior over directed acyclic graphs. The obvious implementation enumerates them, and enumeration is a wall rather than a slope: 543 graphs at d=4, 29,281 at d=5, 3,781,503 at d=6, and 1.14 billion at d=7. Every quantity the pipeline reads stops existing one node past the current setting.

That wall is now gone. A dynamic program over subsets of nodes computes the same quantities exactly, in time proportional to 3d rather than to the number of graphs, and all edge marginals come from a single reverse-mode sweep instead of d(d−1) separate runs.

Two further pieces were needed before a d=7 result could mean anything, because both the greedy baseline and Gate 1 also read the graph list. The oracle needs the distribution over which nodes are downstream of an intervention, which does not decompose and so is supplied by a Metropolis-Hastings sampler verified directly against the exact posterior. Gate 1’s target is now computed by a local test — a graph is alone in its equivalence class exactly when it has no covered edge — which agrees with the enumerated grouping on all 3,781,503 graphs at d=6.

Corrections

Recorded because they change how much weight the results can carry, and because two of them are methodological rather than incidental.

Verifying against ground truth is not enough if the inputs are unrepresentative

The subset-DP posterior was verified against enumeration at four graph sizes, agreeing to twelve decimal places, and passed 29 tests. It then returned zero on the first real environment episode. The tests used independent random data; the failure mode requires correlated data, which is the only kind a causal discovery environment produces. The acceptance test was right and the test data was wrong. Every test now uses data generated by an actual structural causal model.

Check a new component directly, never through something that consumes it

A graph sampler was evaluated for three rounds by how well the oracle built on top of it performed. From there a correctness bug and slow mixing are indistinguishable, and improving the mixing looked like progress. One direct comparison against the exact posterior settled it immediately, and the sampler was discarded.

Also corrected

  • An oracle-agreement metric that was 93–98% vacuous. It counted a choice as optimal whenever the oracle had no preference — which is most steps. Agreement is now aggregated only over steps where the oracle actually discriminates.
  • A pre-registered acceptance test that was badly designed. “The estimate’s confidence interval must contain the true value” fails 5% of the time by construction; across nine configurations that is a 37% chance of a spurious failure. One duly failed, by 0.00025. Replaced with a test of the claim actually being made — that the estimator is unbiased — which it passes with a maximum z-score of 1.86.
  • A runtime projection built by mixing two machines. A cluster baseline was scaled by a speedup ratio measured on a laptop, giving 2.7 hours against an actual 4.14. The first diagnosis of the discrepancy was also wrong — blamed on slower cluster cores, when the cluster is in fact faster and the real cause was a different episode count.
  • A speedup threshold that is no longer met where it was set. The one-pass edge-marginal computation was required to be 5× faster at d=6 and reached 6.4×. The numerically-safe rewrite brought that to 3.7× at d=6, meeting 5× only from d=7 on. The threshold was re-anchored to d=7, which is a moved goalpost and is labelled as one.

Where this leaves the thesis

What can be claimed

On environments proved to require experimentation, a learned policy identifies causal graphs in fewer experiments than the myopic expected-information-gain oracle, at three graph sizes, on all 23 seeds run, robustly across the entire hyperparameter surface swept. This is possible in principle because expected information gain is not adaptively submodular, so chaining single-step optima carries no optimality guarantee — the headroom the agent finds is theoretically expected, and the contribution is showing it is reachable by learning.

What cannot

  • The oracle is optimistic: it assumes an experiment reveals its outcome perfectly, so it is credited with distinctions a finite-sample agent could not make. Beating it is meaningful; the margin is not a clean measure of planning quality.
  • Sizes reach d=6 with results, and the infrastructure now reaches d=9. The trend of a narrowing margin should not be extrapolated past what was measured.
  • “Matches but never beats” was fixed in advance as a valid finding rather than a failure. It did not occur, which is worth stating precisely because the alternative was pre-committed.

Next: two agents

The two-agent design splits six nodes into two private pairs and two shared exposed nodes, with edges between the private sets forbidden — neither agent could ever observe such an edge, so allowing it would make the graph unrecoverable by construction.

An exhaustive measurement over that space produced the finding that matters most for the design. In 43.9% of graphs at least one agent’s view contains an unobserved common cause, so a local DAG model is misspecified on nearly half of instances. The candidate topology built to remove that problem does remove it completely — and simultaneously drops the share of ambiguity sitting at the federation boundary from 66.9% to zero. It buys a well-specified local model by deleting the phenomenon the two-agent case exists to study, so it has been rejected.

That turns the risk into the motivation: on 44% of instances, the only way for an agent to be right about its own neighbourhood is to learn something about a variable it is not allowed to see. That is a precise structural reason why coordination is necessary rather than merely helpful.

And coordination is worth sequencing, not sharing

A second gate asks whether coordination buys anything at all: two agents choosing independently from their own local views, against one centralised chooser, with inference held identical and budgets matched in interventions. Centralised needs 1.74 interventions against 2.52, intervals disjoint.

The interesting part is a decomposition that emerged from getting it wrong first. When the centralised chooser had to commit to both interventions simultaneously — matching the independent agents’ cadence, so that only the shared information differed — it did not win: 2.80 against 2.64. The advantage appears only once it can re-plan between interventions. So under this topology the value of coordination is almost entirely the ability to sequence experiments, not the shared belief, which points the design at turn-taking over the shared nodes rather than at belief-sharing protocols.
